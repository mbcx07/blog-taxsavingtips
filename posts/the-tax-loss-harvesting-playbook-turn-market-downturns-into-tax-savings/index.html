The Tax-Loss Harvesting Playbook: Turn Market Downturns into Tax Savings

2026-07-05 · Personal Tax · Tags: personal tax, taxsavingtips

When the stock market dips, most investors feel a pang of anxiety. But for the tax-savvy individual, a downturn is more than just a temporary setback—it’s an opportunity. Tax-loss harvesting, a strategy often overlooked by everyday investors, allows you to turn paper losses into real, tangible tax savings. By strategically selling underperforming investments and using those losses to offset gains—and even ordinary income—you can reduce your tax bill while keeping your portfolio aligned with your long-term goals. In this guide, I’ll walk you through the mechanics, the timing, and the pitfalls of tax-loss harvesting, so you can make the most of every market dip without breaking the rules.

What Is Tax-Loss Harvesting and Why Should You Care?

At its core, tax-loss harvesting is the practice of selling an investment that has declined in value since you purchased it, thereby realizing a capital loss. That loss can then be used to offset capital gains you’ve realized elsewhere in your portfolio. If your losses exceed your gains, you can use up to $3,000 of the excess to reduce your ordinary taxable income each year (or $1,500 if you’re married filing separately). Any remaining losses can be carried forward indefinitely to offset future gains.

Why does this matter? Because capital gains taxes can take a significant bite out of your investment returns. For example, if you’re in the 22% tax bracket and you sell a stock for a $5,000 gain, you’ll owe $1,100 in federal taxes. But if you also have a $5,000 loss from another stock, that gain is completely wiped out—saving you that $1,100. Even better, if you have no gains to offset, that $3,000 loss deduction could lower your taxable income, potentially saving you hundreds of dollars on your tax return. Over time, these savings compound, giving you more money to reinvest and grow.

The Mechanics: How to Harvest Losses Without Derailing Your Strategy

Implementing tax-loss harvesting isn’t just about selling losers—it’s about doing so in a way that maintains your investment strategy. The key is to sell the losing position and then replace it with a similar, but not identical, investment to keep your asset allocation intact. For instance, if you sell shares of an S&P 500 index fund at a loss, you might immediately buy shares of a total stock market index fund or a large-cap value fund. This way, you stay invested in the market while locking in the tax benefit.

Here’s a step-by-step approach to the process:

  • Identify positions in your taxable brokerage account that are currently trading below your cost basis (the price you paid, including commissions).
  • Review your portfolio for any realized capital gains from other sales during the year—these are the gains you want to offset.
  • Sell the losing positions, making sure to document the sale date and the loss amount.
  • Immediately (or within a few days) purchase a replacement investment that is not “substantially identical” to the one you sold, to avoid the wash sale rule.
  • Keep records of all transactions for your tax filing.

One common misconception is that you must wait until December to harvest losses. While year-end is a popular time, you can harvest losses at any point during the year. In fact, harvesting earlier gives you more flexibility to offset gains that may arise later. Many investors do a mid-year review to capture losses before they’re forgotten.

The Wash Sale Rule: The Trap That Can Invalidate Your Losses

If there’s one rule that trips up even seasoned investors, it’s the wash sale rule. The IRS prohibits you from claiming a loss on a sale if you buy a “substantially identical” security within 30 days before or after the sale. This includes the day of the sale itself. If you violate this rule, the loss is disallowed for the current tax year, and your cost basis in the new shares is adjusted, effectively deferring the loss to a future date.

What counts as “substantially identical”? It’s not always clear-cut. For example, selling shares of one mutual fund and buying shares of a different fund that tracks the same index might be considered substantially identical if the funds are nearly identical in composition and objective. To be safe, choose a replacement fund that tracks a different index or has a different investment style. For individual stocks, selling Apple and buying Microsoft is clearly not identical, but selling Apple and buying Apple call options could be problematic.

Another nuance: the wash sale rule applies to your entire portfolio, not just the account you’re trading in. If you sell a losing stock in your taxable account and buy the same stock in your IRA within the 30-day window, the loss is still disallowed. So be mindful of all your accounts when planning your trades.

Advanced Strategies: Pairing Tax-Loss Harvesting with Tax-Gain Harvesting

While tax-loss harvesting is powerful, it’s only half the story. The flip side is tax-gain harvesting—selling investments that have appreciated in value to realize gains, but only when your tax rate is low. For example, if you’re in the 0% long-term capital gains bracket (which applies to single filers with taxable income up to $47,025 in 2025, and married couples up to $94,050), you can sell winning investments and pay no federal tax on the gains. This allows you to reset your cost basis higher, reducing future taxable gains when you eventually sell.

Combining both strategies can be particularly effective. Suppose you have a losing stock and a winning stock. You could sell the loser to offset the gain from the winner, but if you’re in the 0% bracket, you might want to realize the gain without offsetting it, and instead use the loss to offset ordinary income. The key is to model your tax situation each year to determine the optimal approach. For most people, tax-loss harvesting is the priority, but if you have room in the 0% bracket, tax-gain harvesting can be a valuable addition.

Another advanced tactic is to use losses to offset not just capital gains but also up to $3,000 of ordinary income. This is especially beneficial if you have a high salary and are in a higher tax bracket. For example, if you’re in the 32% bracket, a $3,000 loss deduction saves you $960 in federal taxes. That’s a meaningful amount that can be reinvested.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Harvesting Losses

Even with the best intentions, investors often make errors that undermine the benefits of tax-loss harvesting. Here are the most frequent pitfalls I see as a CPA:

  • Ignoring transaction costs: If you’re trading frequently, commissions and bid-ask spreads can eat into your savings. Make sure the tax benefit outweighs the costs.
  • Harvesting losses in tax-advantaged accounts: Losses in IRAs or 401(k)s don’t provide any tax benefit because gains and losses within these accounts are not taxed until withdrawal. Only harvest losses in taxable brokerage accounts.
  • Forgetting about state taxes: While federal rules allow up to $3,000 in ordinary income offset, some states have different rules. Check your state’s treatment of capital losses to avoid surprises.
  • Being too aggressive with replacements: Buying a replacement that is too similar can trigger the wash sale rule. Always err on the side of caution and choose a fund with a different benchmark or a stock in a different sector.
  • Not tracking your cost basis correctly: With the new cost basis reporting rules, brokers report your basis to the IRS, but you’re still responsible for accuracy. Keep your own records, especially if you’ve made multiple purchases at different prices.

One more mistake: harvesting losses in December without considering your full-year picture. If you’ve already realized significant gains earlier in the year, you might want to harvest losses earlier to offset them. Waiting until December could mean missing the opportunity if the market rebounds and your losses shrink.

Putting It All Together: A Year-Round Tax-Loss Harvesting Plan

Tax-loss harvesting isn’t a one-time event—it’s a year-round discipline. Here’s how to integrate it into your financial routine:

First, set a reminder to review your portfolio quarterly. Look for any positions that are down more than 10% from your cost basis. If you have gains elsewhere, consider harvesting those losses to offset them. If you don’t have gains, you can still harvest losses to take the $3,000 deduction, but be mindful of the wash sale rule if you plan to repurchase the same security later.

Second, keep a running list of your realized gains and losses throughout the year. This helps you make informed decisions as new opportunities arise. Many brokerage platforms now offer tax-loss harvesting tools that automatically identify losses and suggest replacements, but you should still understand the logic behind them.

Finally, coordinate with your overall tax plan. If you’re expecting a large capital gain from a real estate sale or a business exit, you can use tax-loss harvesting to offset that gain. Conversely, if you’re in a low-income year, you might want to delay harvesting losses to a future year when you’ll be in a higher bracket. The key is to be proactive, not reactive.

Conclusion

Tax-loss harvesting is one of the most underutilized tools in the personal tax playbook. It’s legal, straightforward, and can save you thousands of dollars over time. By turning market volatility into a tax advantage, you not only reduce your current tax bill but also set yourself up for greater long-term growth. Remember to respect the wash sale rule, choose replacement investments wisely, and integrate this strategy into your broader financial plan. As with any tax strategy, consult with a qualified tax professional to ensure you’re making the most of your unique situation. The next time the market dips, don’t just worry—harvest.
