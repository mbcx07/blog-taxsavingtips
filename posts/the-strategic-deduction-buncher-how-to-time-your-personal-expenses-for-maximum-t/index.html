The Strategic Deduction Buncher: How to Time Your Personal Expenses for Maximum Tax Savings

2026-05-29 · Personal Tax · Tags: personal tax, taxsavingtips

When it comes to personal taxes, most people think the only way to save is to find every possible deduction and credit. But there's a more powerful, often overlooked strategy that can dramatically increase your tax savings without changing your spending habits. It's called deduction bunching, and it's the art of timing your deductible expenses to concentrate them into a single tax year, allowing you to itemize when it benefits you most and take the standard deduction in other years. As a CPA, I've seen clients slash their tax bills by thousands of dollars simply by shifting when they pay certain expenses. In this guide, I'll show you exactly how to implement this strategy, which deductions are best suited for bunching, and how to avoid common pitfalls.

The Standard Deduction vs. Itemizing: The Foundation

To understand bunching, you first need to grasp the basic trade-off between the standard deduction and itemized deductions. For the 2025 tax year, the standard deduction is $15,000 for single filers and $30,000 for married couples filing jointly. If your total itemized deductions—things like mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable contributions, and medical expenses—exceed your standard deduction, you'll itemize and deduct the larger amount. If not, you take the standard deduction and forgo the itemized benefits.

The problem is that many taxpayers hover right around the standard deduction threshold. In one year, they might have $14,000 in itemized deductions, missing the standard deduction by just $1,000. The next year, they might have $16,000, but the standard deduction has also increased, so they still don't benefit. This is where bunching comes in. By concentrating your deductible expenses into one year, you can push your itemized total well above the standard deduction, reaping a significant tax benefit in that year, while taking the standard deduction in the off years. Over a two-year cycle, you often end up deducting more than you would have by simply itemizing every year.

How Bunching Works: A Simple Example

Let's say you're married and file jointly. Your typical itemized deductions include $8,000 in state income taxes, $5,000 in charitable contributions, and $4,000 in property taxes—totaling $17,000. That's below the $30,000 standard deduction, so you'd take the standard deduction and lose the benefit of those itemized expenses. But what if you could shift some of those expenses into a single year? For instance, you could make two years' worth of charitable contributions in one year—$10,000 instead of $5,000—and prepay your property taxes for the next year in December, adding another $4,000. Now your itemized total for that year is $8,000 (state taxes) + $10,000 (charity) + $8,000 (property taxes) = $26,000, still below $30,000. But if you also have a medical expense or mortgage interest, you might cross the threshold. The key is to plan so that in the "bunched" year, your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction by a wide margin, and in the "off" year, you take the standard deduction with minimal itemized expenses.

The real magic happens over multiple years. In the bunched year, you deduct, say, $35,000. In the off year, you deduct $30,000 (standard). That's $65,000 over two years, versus $60,000 if you had itemized $30,000 each year (which you couldn't because your actual expenses were only $17,000 per year). The savings can be substantial, especially if you're in a higher tax bracket.

Charitable Contributions: The Perfect Bunching Candidate

Charitable giving is one of the easiest deductions to bunch because you have complete control over when you donate. Instead of giving $1,000 every year, you can give $2,000 every other year. This works beautifully with donor-advised funds (DAFs), which allow you to contribute a large sum in one year, receive an immediate tax deduction, and then recommend grants to your favorite charities over time. For example, you could contribute $10,000 to a DAF in December of 2025, deduct that full amount on your 2025 return, and then distribute $5,000 to charities in 2026 and $5,000 in 2027. This way, you get the tax benefit upfront while still supporting causes you care about on your own timeline.

Another strategy is to donate appreciated assets, such as stocks or mutual funds, instead of cash. When you donate appreciated securities held for more than a year, you avoid paying capital gains tax on the appreciation and can deduct the full fair market value. Bunching these donations into a single year amplifies the tax benefit. Just be sure to follow the IRS rules: you need a written acknowledgment from the charity for any single contribution of $250 or more, and for non-cash donations over $500, you'll need to file Form 8283.

Medical Expenses and State Taxes: Timing Your Payments

Medical expenses are deductible only to the extent they exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income (AGI). This makes them a natural candidate for bunching because you can schedule elective procedures, dental work, and even prescription purchases to fall in the same year. For example, if you know you need a new pair of glasses, a dental crown, or a physical therapy session, you can delay or accelerate those expenses to align with your bunched year. Keep in mind that you can only deduct medical expenses you actually paid during the tax year, not just incurred. So if you have a credit card bill, the deduction is allowed when you pay the bill, not when you charge it.

State and local taxes (SALT) are also deductible, but there's a $10,000 cap ($5,000 if married filing separately) on the combined deduction for state income taxes, property taxes, and sales taxes. This cap makes bunching less effective for SALT because you can't deduct more than the cap in any year. However, you can still time your property tax payments. If you pay your property taxes in January instead of December, you shift the deduction to the next year. Many taxpayers prepay their January property tax bill in December to bunch it into the current year, but be aware of the SALT cap—if you're already at the cap, prepaying won't help. Also, the IRS has rules about prepaying state income taxes; you can only deduct state income taxes that are actually withheld or paid during the year, so you can't prepay your state income tax for next year.

Other Deductions and Credits to Consider

While bunching works best for charitable contributions and medical expenses, there are other deductions and credits that can be strategically timed. For instance, the American Opportunity Tax Credit (AOTC) for education expenses is worth up to $2,500 per student, but it's only available for the first four years of post-secondary education. If you have a child in college, you can pay tuition for the spring semester in December to claim the credit in the current year, or pay in January to shift it to the next year. Similarly, the Lifetime Learning Credit (LLC) can be used for any year of post-secondary education, but it's non-refundable and has income limits. Timing your tuition payments can help you maximize these credits in a year when your income is lower.

Another often-overlooked deduction is the home office deduction, but that's only for self-employed individuals. For employees, the home office deduction was suspended with the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act. However, if you have a side business, you can bunch business expenses like equipment purchases, supplies, and even a new computer into a single year to maximize your business deductions, which can offset your personal income. Just be sure to follow the rules for business use and keep proper documentation.

Finally, consider the timing of your income. If you expect a higher income in one year, you might want to accelerate deductions into that year to offset the higher tax rate. Conversely, if you expect a lower income, you might defer deductions to a year when they'll be more valuable. This is a more advanced strategy, but it works hand-in-hand with bunching. For example, if you're retiring next year and your income will drop, you might want to bunch deductions into the current year when your tax rate is higher.

Putting It All Together: A Year-Round Plan

Bunching isn't a one-time decision; it's a year-round planning process. Start by estimating your itemized deductions for the current year and the next year. Use tax software or a simple spreadsheet to project your AGI, standard deduction, and potential itemized deductions. Then, identify which expenses you can control—charitable contributions, medical procedures, property tax payments, and tuition. Decide which year you want to be your "bunched" year, and then schedule those expenses accordingly. For example, if you're planning a bunched year in 2025, you might make your 2025 and 2026 charitable contributions in December 2025, schedule elective surgery in early 2025, and prepay your January 2026 property tax in December 2025. In 2026, you'll take the standard deduction and keep your itemized expenses minimal.

One important caveat: the IRS has anti-abuse rules, particularly for prepaid expenses. For example, you can't deduct prepaid interest on a mortgage (points) unless they meet specific criteria. Also, the IRS requires that charitable contributions be made to qualified organizations and that you have proper documentation. And for medical expenses, you can only deduct amounts paid for medical care, not for future services. So, while bunching is legal and effective, you must ensure that your timing is genuine and not just a paper transaction.

Another tip: if you're over 70½, you can make qualified charitable distributions (QCDs) from your IRA directly to a charity, up to $100,000 per year. These QCDs count toward your required minimum distributions (RMDs) and are excluded from your taxable income, but they don't count as itemized deductions. However, you can still bunch your QCDs into a single year to reduce your AGI, which can help you stay under certain income thresholds for other deductions and credits. This is a powerful strategy for retirees.

Conclusion

Deduction bunching is one of the most effective yet underutilized strategies in personal tax planning. By shifting the timing of your deductible expenses, you can turn a year of near-miss itemizing into a year of significant tax savings. The key is to plan ahead, understand the standard deduction thresholds, and focus on expenses you can control—charitable contributions, medical costs, and property taxes. With a little effort, you can potentially save thousands of dollars over a two-year cycle, all while staying perfectly within IRS rules. As a CPA, I encourage you to review your own deduction patterns and consider whether bunching makes sense for your situation. If you're unsure, consult with a tax professional who can help you model different scenarios and implement a strategy that fits your financial goals. Remember, the best tax strategy is one that's proactive, not reactive—and bunching is the perfect example of that mindset.
