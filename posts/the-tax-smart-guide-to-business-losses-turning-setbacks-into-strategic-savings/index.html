The Tax-Smart Guide to Business Losses: Turning Setbacks into Strategic Savings

2026-07-01 · Planning · Tags: The, planning, taxsavingtips

Every business owner knows the sting of a bad year. Sales dip, unexpected expenses pile up, and suddenly you're staring at a loss on your profit and loss statement. While it's natural to view a loss as a failure, the tax code actually offers a silver lining: business losses can be transformed into valuable tax savings—if you know how to handle them strategically. As a CPA, I've seen too many entrepreneurs leave money on the table by mishandling their losses, either by failing to document them properly or by not understanding the complex rules that govern how and when they can be used. In this guide, I'll walk you through the ins and outs of business losses, from the basics of net operating losses to advanced planning techniques that can turn a tough year into a tax advantage.

Understanding Business Losses and Net Operating Losses

At its core, a business loss occurs when your deductible business expenses exceed your business income for the year. For sole proprietors, single-member LLCs, and partnerships, this loss typically flows through to your personal tax return, potentially offsetting other income like wages, interest, or dividends. For corporations, the loss stays at the entity level. But the real power lies in what happens when your total deductions exceed your total income across all sources—that's when you create a net operating loss (NOL).

An NOL is essentially a negative taxable income that can be used to reduce your tax liability in other years. The concept is simple: the IRS allows you to smooth out the ups and downs of business cycles by applying losses from bad years against profits from good years. However, the rules governing NOLs have changed significantly in recent years, and understanding the current landscape is crucial for effective planning.

The Current NOL Rules: Carryforward, Carryback, and the 80% Limitation

If you're dealing with a loss from a tax year beginning after December 31, 2020, the rules are straightforward but restrictive. Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) and subsequent modifications, NOLs can be carried forward indefinitely—no more 20-year expiration—but they can only offset up to 80% of your taxable income in any given carryforward year. This means if you have a $100,000 NOL and your taxable income in a future year is $100,000, you can only use $80,000 of the loss, leaving $20,000 to carry forward again.

What about carrybacks? For most businesses, the ability to carry back losses to prior years was eliminated by the TCJA. The CARES Act temporarily allowed a five-year carryback for losses arising in 2018, 2019, and 2020, but that provision has expired. As of 2025, unless Congress acts again, you cannot carry back a loss to a prior year. This means you must plan for the future, not the past, when you have a loss year.

There are exceptions, though. Certain farming businesses and small businesses with average gross receipts of $25 million or less may still be eligible for a two-year carryback. But for the vast majority of small business owners, the forward-only approach is the new normal. This makes timing and forecasting even more critical.

Strategic Planning with Business Losses: Timing Income and Expenses

Given the 80% limitation and the lack of carryback, the key to maximizing your loss utilization is to control when you recognize income and expenses. If you anticipate a loss this year, you might want to accelerate income into the current year to reduce the size of the loss, or defer expenses to a future year when you expect to have profits. Conversely, if you're in a profitable year and have a loss carryforward, you might want to defer income to a later year to stay under the 80% threshold—but that's often counterproductive because you'd be paying tax now instead of later.

A more effective strategy is to use the loss to offset income from other sources. For example, if you have a side business that's losing money, you can use that loss to reduce your W-2 wages, provided you meet the passive activity rules (more on that below). This can result in immediate tax savings, even if the business itself isn't profitable. The key is to ensure the loss is legitimate and that you have proper documentation to support it.

Another angle: if you're close to the 80% limit in a carryforward year, consider making additional retirement contributions or increasing other deductions to lower your taxable income further, thereby reducing the amount of loss you need to use. This preserves more of your NOL for future years when your tax rate might be higher.

Navigating Passive Activity and At-Risk Rules

Not all business losses are created equal. The IRS imposes two major restrictions that can limit your ability to deduct losses: the passive activity loss (PAL) rules and the at-risk rules. The PAL rules apply to losses from activities in which you do not materially participate. If you're a passive investor in a business—say, a limited partner or a silent investor—you generally cannot deduct losses against active income like wages or business profits. Instead, those losses are suspended and carried forward until you either dispose of the activity or begin to materially participate.

The at-risk rules are even more fundamental. You can only deduct losses up to the amount you have at risk in the business—that is, money you've invested plus any debt for which you're personally liable. If you've borrowed money from the business or used nonrecourse financing, you may not be able to claim losses beyond your actual economic exposure. This is a common trap for real estate investors and small business owners who rely heavily on borrowed capital.

To avoid these pitfalls, you need to carefully structure your involvement and financing. If you want to deduct losses, you must be actively involved in the day-to-day operations and have personal skin in the game. Keep detailed records of your time and participation, and make sure your loans are structured as recourse debt if you intend to claim losses.

Documentation and Substantiation: The Key to Defending Your Losses

The IRS is notoriously aggressive when it comes to business losses, especially for sole proprietors and small businesses. A loss on your tax return is a red flag that can trigger an audit. To protect yourself, you need impeccable documentation. This means keeping a separate bank account for your business, maintaining a mileage log if you use your vehicle, and retaining receipts for every expense you claim. But beyond that, you need to be able to prove that your business is operated with the intent to make a profit. The IRS uses the “hobby loss” rules to disallow deductions from activities that are not engaged in for profit. If you have a history of losses, you must be able to show that you're making a genuine effort to turn things around—by developing a business plan, seeking professional advice, and adjusting your strategies.

One practical tip: create a loss utilization tracker. This is a simple spreadsheet that records your NOL carryforward amounts, the year they originated, and how much you've used each year. This will help you avoid missing the 80% limitation and ensure you don't accidentally let a loss expire (though they no longer expire, it's still good practice). Also, keep all correspondence with tax professionals and any financial projections you've made—these can be powerful evidence of your profit motive.

Special Situations: Startups, Partnerships, and S-Corps

If you're a startup, you may face unique challenges. The IRS has special rules for “start-up expenditures” that allow you to deduct up to $5,000 in the first year, with the rest amortized over 15 years. But if your startup generates a loss, you may be subject to the at-risk and passive activity rules, especially if you're not yet actively involved. For partnerships and S-corporations, losses flow through to owners, but they are limited by the owner's basis in the entity. If you don't have enough basis, the loss is suspended until you contribute more capital or the entity generates income. This is a common issue for S-corp shareholders who take small salaries but have large losses—they may not be able to deduct the full amount.

To maximize your ability to use these losses, consider making a capital contribution to the entity before year-end, or restructuring your debt to increase your basis. For example, if you're an S-corp shareholder, you can lend money to the corporation, which increases your basis and allows you to deduct more losses. Just be sure to document the loan properly and charge a reasonable interest rate to avoid recharacterization.

Conclusion

Business losses are not a sign of failure—they're a natural part of the entrepreneurial journey. But how you handle them can make a significant difference to your bottom line. By understanding the current NOL rules, strategically timing your income and expenses, navigating the passive activity and at-risk limitations, and maintaining meticulous documentation, you can turn a difficult year into a tax-saving opportunity. As always, consult with a qualified tax professional who can help you tailor these strategies to your specific situation. Remember, the goal isn't just to survive a loss—it's to use it as a springboard for future success. With the right planning, you can ensure that your setbacks become stepping stones to greater tax efficiency and long-term wealth.
