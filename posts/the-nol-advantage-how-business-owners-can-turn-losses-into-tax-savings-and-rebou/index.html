The NOL Advantage: How Business Owners Can Turn Losses into Tax Savings and Rebound Stronger

2026-06-25 · Business Tax · Tags: taxsavingtips, business tax, The

For many business owners, a losing year feels like a failure. But in the world of tax strategy, a net operating loss (NOL) is not a scarlet letter—it’s a financial tool that can reduce your tax burden, smooth out cash flow, and even fund your recovery. The key is understanding how NOLs work and how to deploy them strategically. As a CPA who has guided countless entrepreneurs through downturns, I’ve seen firsthand how a well-planned NOL strategy can turn a red-ink year into a springboard for future growth. In this guide, I’ll walk you through the mechanics of NOLs, the current tax rules, and the smart moves that can help you maximize this often-overlooked tax break.

What Exactly Is a Net Operating Loss?

A net operating loss occurs when your business’s deductible expenses exceed its gross income for a tax year. For example, if your company earns $200,000 in revenue but incurs $250,000 in allowable deductions—including cost of goods sold, salaries, rent, marketing, and depreciation—you have a $50,000 NOL. This loss isn’t just a number on your profit-and-loss statement; it’s a tax asset that can offset taxable income in other years, reducing your overall tax liability.

It’s important to distinguish an NOL from a simple business loss. A business loss might be limited by passive activity rules or at-risk limitations, but an NOL is specifically calculated after applying those rules. For pass-through entities like sole proprietorships, LLCs, and S-corporations, the NOL flows through to your personal tax return. For C-corporations, the NOL is computed at the corporate level. Understanding which entity type you operate under is crucial because it affects how you claim the loss and how it interacts with your other income.

The Mechanics of NOLs: Carryback and Carryforward Rules

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017 dramatically changed NOL rules, and the CARES Act temporarily modified them. As of the 2025 tax year, the rules are as follows: For NOLs arising in tax years beginning after December 31, 2020, you cannot carry the loss back to prior years. Instead, you can carry it forward indefinitely, but the deduction is limited to 80% of your taxable income in any given carryforward year. This means if you have a $100,000 NOL and your taxable income in a future year is $50,000, you can only use $40,000 of the NOL to offset that income, leaving the remaining $60,000 to carry forward.

For NOLs arising in 2018, 2019, or 2020, the CARES Act allowed a five-year carryback and temporarily suspended the 80% limitation, making those losses fully deductible. If you have any unused NOLs from those years, you may still be able to amend prior returns or file for a quick refund using Form 1139 (for corporations) or Form 1045 (for individuals). The key takeaway: don’t assume your old NOLs are worthless—they might be more valuable than you think.

Strategically, the carryforward rule creates a planning opportunity. If you expect your income to rise in the coming years, you can “bank” your NOLs to offset that higher income when you’re in a higher tax bracket. Conversely, if you have a low-income year ahead, you might want to accelerate income or defer deductions to use up NOLs before they expire—though they never expire, so there’s no urgency. The real art is matching your NOL usage with your projected tax rate.

Strategic Use of NOLs: Timing and Tax Rate Arbitrage

One of the most powerful ways to use an NOL is to time its application to years when your marginal tax rate is highest. For example, if you’re a sole proprietor in the 24% bracket this year but expect to be in the 35% bracket next year due to a new contract, you might want to defer using the NOL until next year. However, the 80% limitation means you can’t use it all at once, so you’ll need to plan a multi-year strategy.

Another tactic is to accelerate income into a year when you have an NOL to offset it. For instance, if you have a $50,000 NOL and you’re about to invoice a client for $50,000, consider sending the invoice before year-end so the income is taxed at zero (or reduced) rather than waiting until the next year when the NOL may be partially used. This is a classic “income shifting” move that requires careful coordination with your accountant.

For C-corporations, NOLs can be particularly valuable because corporate tax rates are flat at 21% (as of 2025). If your corporation has an NOL, it can offset future profits, effectively giving you a tax-free year. But beware of the 80% limitation—you’ll still owe tax on 20% of your taxable income. To maximize the benefit, consider accelerating deductions into the NOL year to increase the loss, or deferring income to a year when you can fully utilize the carryforward.

NOLs and Business Structure: Pass-Through vs. C-Corp

Your business structure determines how NOLs are treated. In a sole proprietorship, LLC, or S-corporation, the NOL flows through to your personal return and can offset other income—such as wages, interest, or your spouse’s salary—subject to the 80% limitation. This can be a lifeline if you have a side business that loses money while you work a full-time job. However, the IRS has strict rules about “hobby losses” and passive activity losses, so you must ensure your business is operated with a profit motive and that you materially participate.

For S-corporations, there’s an additional wrinkle: the NOL is limited to your basis in the stock and debt. If your basis is insufficient, the loss is suspended and carried forward until you contribute more capital or the company generates income. This is a common trap—business owners think they can deduct losses, but they can’t if they haven’t invested enough. Planning ahead by making capital contributions or loans can unlock those suspended losses.

C-corporations, on the other hand, have no such basis limitations. The NOL is a corporate asset that can be carried forward indefinitely, but it’s trapped inside the corporation. If you later sell the corporation, the NOL may be lost or limited under Section 382. That’s why it’s essential to consider your exit strategy when deciding how to use NOLs.

Documentation and Compliance: Protecting Your NOL Claims

The IRS scrutinizes NOL deductions, so meticulous documentation is non-negotiable. You need to maintain records that prove the loss occurred, including profit and loss statements, expense receipts, bank statements, and any relevant contracts. If you’re claiming an NOL on your tax return, you must file Form 1045 (for individuals) or Form 1139 (for corporations) to apply for a quick refund, or you can simply carry it forward on future returns. The carryforward is tracked on Schedule A of Form 1040 for individuals, or on the corporate return’s NOL schedule.

One common mistake is failing to track NOLs across multiple years. The IRS requires you to maintain a detailed NOL schedule that shows the year the loss arose, the amount used each year, and the remaining balance. If you’re audited, you’ll need to produce this schedule. I recommend creating a separate spreadsheet or using tax software that tracks NOLs automatically. Also, be aware of the 80% limitation—if you don’t apply it correctly, you could underpay your taxes and face penalties.

Another compliance issue is the interaction between NOLs and other tax attributes, such as capital losses, charitable contributions, and the qualified business income deduction. For example, an NOL can reduce your adjusted gross income, which in turn affects your ability to deduct charitable contributions or claim certain credits. A skilled tax professional can help you sequence these deductions to maximize your overall savings.

Conclusion

A net operating loss is not a sign of failure—it’s a strategic opportunity. By understanding the current carryback and carryforward rules, timing your NOL usage to align with your highest tax rates, and ensuring proper documentation, you can turn a difficult year into a powerful tax advantage. Whether you’re a sole proprietor, an S-corp shareholder, or a C-corp owner, the NOL rules offer flexibility that can help you rebound stronger. But don’t wait until tax season to think about this. Work with your CPA now to project your income, estimate your NOLs, and build a multi-year plan. The tax code rewards those who plan ahead, and NOLs are one of the most valuable rewards available to business owners. So embrace the red ink—it might just be the black ink of your future.
