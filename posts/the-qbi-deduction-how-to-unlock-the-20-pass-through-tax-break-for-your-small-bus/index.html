The QBI Deduction: How to Unlock the 20% Pass-Through Tax Break for Your Small Business

2026-06-30 · Deductions · Tags: deductions, The, taxsavingtips

If you own a small business structured as a sole proprietorship, partnership, LLC, or S-corporation, you may be leaving a significant tax break on the table. The Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction, introduced by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, allows eligible pass-through business owners to deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income from their taxable income. Unlike many deductions that require itemizing or tracking expenses, the QBI deduction is a straightforward reduction that can lower your effective tax rate without a single receipt. Yet, it remains one of the most misunderstood and underutilized tax strategies. In this guide, I’ll walk you through how the QBI deduction works, who qualifies, the critical limitations that can reduce or eliminate it, and practical strategies to maximize your benefit. By the end, you’ll know exactly how to claim this powerful deduction and keep more of your hard-earned profits.

What Is the QBI Deduction and Why Does It Matter?

The QBI deduction, also known as Section 199A, was designed to level the playing field between traditional C-corporations (which pay a 21% corporate tax rate) and pass-through entities (whose income is taxed at individual rates). Before 2018, pass-through owners paid taxes on their full business income at their personal marginal rate, which could be as high as 37%. The QBI deduction effectively reduces the taxable portion of that income by up to 20%, meaning if you earn $100,000 in qualified business income, you only pay tax on $80,000 of it—provided you meet all the requirements.

This deduction is not an itemized deduction; it’s an “above-the-line” deduction that reduces your adjusted gross income (AGI). That means you can claim it even if you take the standard deduction, and it can also lower your eligibility for other tax benefits tied to AGI, such as the child tax credit or Roth IRA contribution limits. For many small business owners, the QBI deduction can translate into thousands of dollars in annual savings—money that can be reinvested into your business or your personal savings.

Who Qualifies for the QBI Deduction?

To claim the QBI deduction, you must have income from a “qualified trade or business” that is passed through to you from a sole proprietorship, partnership, S-corporation, or certain trusts and estates. The deduction is available to U.S. citizens and resident aliens, and it applies to both business income and certain real estate investment income. However, not all pass-through income qualifies. For example, income from a C-corporation, wages earned as an employee, and investment income (like capital gains or dividends) do not count.

There are also specific rules for “specified service trades or businesses” (SSTBs). These include fields such as health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, brokerage services, and any business where the principal asset is the reputation or skill of one or more owners or employees. If your business falls into an SSTB, you may be subject to additional limitations—or completely disqualified from the deduction—if your taxable income exceeds certain thresholds.

For the 2024 tax year, the taxable income thresholds are $383,900 for married filing jointly and $191,950 for single filers. If your taxable income is below these thresholds, you can claim the full 20% deduction on your QBI, regardless of whether you’re an SSTB. Above these thresholds, the deduction begins to phase out for SSTBs, and a separate limitation based on W-2 wages and qualified property kicks in for all businesses.

Understanding the Limitations: W-2 Wages and Qualified Property

Once your taxable income exceeds the threshold, the QBI deduction becomes more complex. For non-SSTB businesses, the deduction is limited to the greater of: (1) 50% of the W-2 wages paid by the business, or (2) 25% of W-2 wages plus 2.5% of the unadjusted basis of qualified property (like buildings and equipment). This limitation is designed to prevent high-income owners from claiming the deduction without having any employees or significant capital investment.

For example, if you own a consulting business (an SSTB) and your taxable income is $400,000 (above the threshold), you may not be able to claim the QBI deduction at all, because the phase-out eliminates it entirely for SSTBs once your income reaches $433,450 (for married filing jointly) or $241,950 (for single). For non-SSTB businesses, the deduction is still available but capped by the wage/property test. This means that a business with no employees and no significant assets will see its QBI deduction reduced or eliminated at higher income levels.

To illustrate: Suppose you own a manufacturing company (non-SSTB) with $500,000 in QBI, and you pay $100,000 in W-2 wages. Your deduction would be limited to 50% of wages, or $50,000, rather than the full 20% of QBI ($100,000). If you also have $1 million in qualified property, the second calculation (25% of wages + 2.5% of property) would be $25,000 + $25,000 = $50,000, so the limitation is the same. In this case, you’d only get a $50,000 deduction, not the full $100,000.

Strategies to Maximize Your QBI Deduction

Given the limitations, proactive planning is essential to maximize your QBI deduction. Here are several strategies that can help you keep more of the deduction, even if you’re above the income thresholds:

  • Consider a W-2 wage strategy: If your business is a sole proprietorship or single-member LLC, you don’t pay yourself a salary—you take draws. But if you’re an S-corporation, you must pay yourself a “reasonable” salary. By increasing your W-2 wages (within reason), you can boost the wage-based limitation and potentially increase your QBI deduction. However, be careful: higher wages also mean higher payroll taxes, so you’ll need to run the numbers.
  • Invest in qualified property: The 2.5% of qualified property component can be a lifeline for capital-intensive businesses. If you’re planning to purchase equipment or real estate, consider doing so before year-end to increase your basis. Even if you use Section 179 or bonus depreciation to expense the cost immediately, the unadjusted basis still counts for QBI purposes, so you can get a double benefit.
  • Split your business into multiple entities: If you have multiple business activities, you might be able to separate them into different entities to avoid the SSTB classification or to keep each entity’s income below the threshold. This is a complex strategy that requires careful legal and tax planning, but it can be effective for high-income professionals.
  • Manage your taxable income: The QBI deduction phases out based on your total taxable income, not just your business income. By deferring income or accelerating deductions (e.g., making retirement contributions, prepaying expenses), you can keep your taxable income below the threshold and preserve the full deduction. For example, contributing to a solo 401(k) or SEP IRA reduces your AGI, which may keep you under the limit.
  • Aggregate your businesses: The IRS allows you to aggregate multiple businesses into a single QBI calculation if they share common ownership and are integrated. This can help you combine W-2 wages and property across entities, potentially increasing your deduction. However, aggregation is an annual election, and you must meet specific criteria.

Common Mistakes to Avoid with the QBI Deduction

Even with the best intentions, many business owners make errors when claiming the QBI deduction. Here are the most common pitfalls I see in my practice:

  • Ignoring the SSTB rules: If you’re in a service business and your income is above the threshold, you might assume you’re not eligible. But the phase-out is gradual, not an all-or-nothing cliff. You may still get a partial deduction if your income is within the phase-out range. Don’t overlook it.
  • Failing to track W-2 wages and property basis: The limitation calculations require accurate records of wages paid and the unadjusted basis of your assets. If you don’t have this information, you might underclaim or overclaim the deduction, leading to IRS scrutiny.
  • Not considering the impact on other deductions: The QBI deduction reduces your AGI, which can affect your eligibility for other tax credits and deductions. For example, a lower AGI might increase your ability to deduct medical expenses or contribute to a Roth IRA. Always model the full tax picture.
  • Assuming all business income qualifies: Income from rental properties may or may not qualify as QBI, depending on how the activity is structured. If you’re a real estate investor, you need to ensure your rental activity rises to the level of a trade or business, which requires regular and continuous involvement.

How to Claim the QBI Deduction on Your Tax Return

Claiming the QBI deduction is relatively straightforward if you use tax software or work with a professional. You’ll need to complete IRS Form 8995-A (or the simpler Form 8995 if your income is below the threshold). The form walks you through the calculation, including your QBI, the wage/property limitation, and any SSTB phase-out. You’ll report the final deduction on Schedule 1 of your Form 1040, line 13.

If you’re a sole proprietor, your QBI is your net profit from Schedule C. For partnerships and S-corporations, you’ll receive a Schedule K-1 that reports your share of QBI. It’s crucial to ensure that your K-1 accurately reflects your QBI, as the entity is responsible for calculating and reporting it. If you have multiple businesses, you’ll need to aggregate them properly, which can be complex. This is one area where working with a tax professional can save you from costly errors.

Conclusion

The QBI deduction is a powerful tool for small business owners, but it’s not a one-size-fits-all benefit. Understanding the nuances—especially the income thresholds, SSTB restrictions, and wage/property limitations—is essential to maximizing your savings. By implementing the strategies outlined above, you can potentially unlock thousands of dollars in tax relief each year. However, because the rules are intricate and change with inflation adjustments, it’s wise to consult with a CPA or tax advisor who can help you navigate the complexities and ensure you’re claiming every dollar you’re entitled to. Don’t leave this deduction on the table—take action today to see if you qualify, and start planning now for next year’s tax bill.
