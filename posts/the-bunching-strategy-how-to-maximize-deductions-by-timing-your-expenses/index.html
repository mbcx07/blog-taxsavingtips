The Bunching Strategy: How to Maximize Deductions by Timing Your Expenses

2026-07-02 · Deductions · Tags: taxsavingtips, deductions, The

For years, the standard deduction was an afterthought for many taxpayers—something you took if you didn't have enough itemized deductions to bother with the paperwork. But the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act changed the game. With the standard deduction now sitting at $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married couples filing jointly (for 2024), millions of Americans find themselves in a strange position: their deductible expenses are significant, but not quite significant enough to surpass that threshold. The result? They lose out on deductions they've genuinely incurred.

If this sounds like your situation, there's a powerful strategy that can help you reclaim those lost deductions without changing your actual spending habits. It's called bunching, and it's one of the most effective—yet underutilized—tax planning techniques available today. By strategically timing when you pay certain deductible expenses, you can concentrate them into a single tax year, push your itemized deductions over the standard deduction threshold, and unlock savings you thought were out of reach. Let me walk you through exactly how it works and how to implement it in your own financial life.

Understanding the Standard Deduction vs. Itemizing

Before we dive into the mechanics of bunching, it's essential to understand the fundamental choice every taxpayer faces. When you file your federal income tax return, you get to choose between taking the standard deduction—a flat amount set by the IRS—or itemizing your deductions, which means listing out each eligible expense you paid during the year. You're allowed to take whichever gives you the larger tax benefit.

For the 2024 tax year, the standard deduction amounts are:

  • Single filers: $14,600
  • Married filing jointly: $29,200
  • Head of household: $21,900

Here's the catch: if your total itemized deductions come to $12,000, you get nothing extra for them. You simply take the $14,600 standard deduction, and those $12,000 in expenses provide zero tax benefit. It's an all-or-nothing proposition. The bunching strategy works by deliberately shifting your deductible expenses between years so that in alternating years, you have enough to itemize, while in the other years, you take the standard deduction. This way, you capture the benefit of your deductions in the itemizing years, rather than letting them go to waste.

Which Deductions Can You Bunch?

Not every deduction is eligible for bunching. The strategy works best with expenses that you have some control over in terms of timing. The most common candidates include:

  • Charitable contributions: You can accelerate or delay donations to your favorite nonprofits. If you're planning to give $5,000 to a charity anyway, you might give $10,000 in one year and nothing the next.
  • Medical expenses: Medical costs are deductible only to the extent they exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income (AGI). Bunching elective procedures, dental work, or purchasing medical equipment in a single year can help you cross that threshold.
  • State and local taxes (SALT): You can prepay your January state income tax estimate in December, or pay your property taxes early if your county allows it. Just be aware of the $10,000 cap on SALT deductions.
  • Miscellaneous itemized deductions: Some investment-related expenses, like certain legal fees or tax preparation costs, can be bunched as well, though these are less common.

What about mortgage interest? Unfortunately, you can't really bunch mortgage interest—it's paid monthly, and prepaying interest is generally not deductible. However, if you're buying a home, you can time your closing to maximize points and interest deductions in a particular year. Similarly, you can't bunch your property tax payments if your local government doesn't allow early payment, so check with your county treasurer's office first.

How to Implement the Bunching Strategy

Now that you understand the concept, let's talk about practical implementation. The first step is to estimate your annual deductible expenses. Look at your spending from the past year and categorize your potential itemized deductions: charitable gifts, medical bills, state and local taxes, and any other deductible expenses. Add them up and compare the total to the standard deduction for your filing status.

If your total is close to—but not quite above—the standard deduction, bunching could work for you. Here's a simple example: Suppose you're married filing jointly, and your typical annual deductions are $18,000 in state and local taxes, $6,000 in charitable contributions, and $3,000 in medical expenses, for a total of $27,000. That's below the $29,200 standard deduction, so you'd take the standard deduction and lose the benefit of those itemized expenses.

Now, let's apply bunching. In Year 1, you prepay your January state income tax estimate in December, bringing your SALT deduction to $20,000. You also accelerate two years' worth of charitable giving—$12,000 instead of $6,000—and schedule an elective medical procedure, bringing your medical total to $5,000. Your Year 1 itemized deductions now total $37,000, well above the standard deduction. You itemize and save taxes on the $7,800 difference.

In Year 2, you have no charitable contributions (you gave them early), your SALT is back to $16,000 (you didn't prepay), and your medical expenses are just $1,000. Your total is $17,000, so you take the standard deduction of $29,200. Over the two-year period, you've deducted $37,000 + $29,200 = $66,200, compared to $27,000 + $29,200 = $56,200 without bunching. That's an extra $10,000 in deductions, which could translate to $2,200 or more in tax savings, depending on your bracket.

Using a Donor-Advised Fund for Charitable Bunching

One of the most elegant tools for bunching charitable contributions is a donor-advised fund (DAF). A DAF is essentially a charitable investment account. You contribute assets—cash, stocks, or even cryptocurrency—to the fund, receive an immediate tax deduction for the full fair market value, and then recommend grants from the fund to your favorite charities over time.

Here's why a DAF is perfect for bunching: you can contribute a large amount in a single year, claim the deduction immediately, and then distribute the funds to charities over the next several years. For example, if you typically give $5,000 annually, you could contribute $15,000 to a DAF in Year 1, deduct the full $15,000, and then direct $5,000 to your favorite causes in each of the next three years. The charities still receive their funding on schedule, but you've concentrated the tax benefit into one year.

This approach is particularly powerful if you have appreciated stocks or other investments. By contributing appreciated assets to a DAF, you avoid paying capital gains tax on the sale, and you get a deduction for the full market value. It's a double tax win. Many brokerage firms offer DAFs with low minimums, making this strategy accessible even for moderate donors.

Bunching Medical Expenses: A Health-Focused Approach

Medical expenses are trickier to bunch because you can't always control when you get sick or need treatment. However, there are legitimate ways to shift medical costs into a single year. If you've been putting off elective procedures—like dental implants, LASIK eye surgery, or a knee replacement—you can schedule them in the same year you expect to have other high medical costs. You can also stock up on prescription medications by getting 90-day supplies in December, or purchase durable medical equipment like hearing aids or a CPAP machine before year-end.

Remember, medical expenses are deductible only to the extent they exceed 7.5% of your AGI. So if your AGI is $100,000, you need medical expenses above $7,500 to get any deduction. Bunching can help you cross that threshold. For example, if you typically have $4,000 in medical costs each year, you're not getting any deduction. But if you bunch two years' worth of expenses—$8,000—into a single year, you can deduct $500 (the amount above $7,500). It's not a huge amount, but combined with other bunched deductions, it can help push you over the standard deduction line.

One caution: don't let the tax tail wag the dog. You should never delay necessary medical care or skip treatments just to save on taxes. But for elective procedures you were planning anyway, bunching is a smart way to maximize the tax benefit.

State and Local Tax Prepayment Strategies

The SALT deduction is capped at $10,000 per year ($5,000 if married filing separately), which limits how much you can bunch here. However, you can still use timing to your advantage. If you live in a state with income taxes, you can prepay your January estimated tax payment in December. Similarly, if your property taxes are due in two installments, you might be able to pay the first half in December and the second half in January, effectively shifting a payment into the earlier tax year.

Just be aware of the alternative minimum tax (AMT) implications. For some taxpayers, prepaying state and local taxes can trigger AMT, which disallows those deductions. It's a complex area, so if you're in a high-income bracket or have significant other deductions, it's worth consulting with a tax professional before implementing this part of the strategy.

Also, be careful about the IRS's rules on prepaid expenses. The IRS has been known to scrutinize prepayments of state income taxes, especially if they're made in December for the following year. As long as the payment is based on a reasonable estimate of your tax liability and your state accepts it, you should be fine. But don't try to prepay more than you actually owe—that could raise red flags.

Making Bunching Work for You: A Year-by-Year Plan

To successfully implement bunching, you need to think in two-year cycles. In your itemizing year, you'll accelerate as many deductible expenses as possible. In your standard deduction year, you'll minimize those expenses. This requires some coordination, especially if you're married and both spouses have charitable giving preferences or medical needs.

Here's a practical approach: Start by creating a spreadsheet of your recurring deductible expenses. Identify which ones you can control and which are fixed. Then, decide which year you want to itemize. For most people, it makes sense to itemize in years when you have a large one-time expense, like a major medical procedure or a big charitable gift. In the other year, you'll take the standard deduction and keep your deductible spending to a minimum.

One important note: bunching doesn't change your total spending over the two-year period. You're still paying the same amount in taxes, charitable contributions, and medical bills. What changes is when you pay them, and that timing shift is what generates the tax savings. It's a pure planning play, not a reduction in your actual expenses.

Finally, remember that tax laws change. The standard deduction amounts are adjusted annually for inflation, and the SALT cap is set to expire after 2025 unless Congress extends it. If you're planning a multi-year bunching strategy, stay informed about current tax law and be ready to adapt. A good tax professional can help you model different scenarios and ensure you're making the most of your deductions without running afoul of IRS rules.

Conclusion

The bunching strategy is one of the most powerful tools in a taxpayer's arsenal, yet it remains surprisingly underused. By simply shifting the timing of your deductible expenses, you can transform deductions that were previously worthless into meaningful tax savings. Whether you're using a donor-advised fund to concentrate charitable gifts, scheduling elective medical procedures strategically, or prepaying state and local taxes, the key is to plan ahead and think in two-year cycles.

Start by evaluating your current deduction situation. If you've been taking the standard deduction year after year while still incurring significant itemized expenses, you're likely leaving money on the table. Bunching can change that. And if you're unsure about the best approach for your specific circumstances, don't hesitate to work with a qualified tax professional who can help you design a strategy that fits your financial goals. With a little forethought, you can turn the standard deduction from a limitation into an opportunity—and keep more of your hard-earned money in your pocket.
