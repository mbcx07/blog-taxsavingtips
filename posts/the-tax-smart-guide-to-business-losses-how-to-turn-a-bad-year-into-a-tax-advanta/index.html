The Tax-Smart Guide to Business Losses: How to Turn a Bad Year into a Tax Advantage

2026-07-21 · Business Tax · Tags: The, taxsavingtips, business tax

Every business owner dreads a losing year. The red ink on your profit-and-loss statement can feel like a personal failure, a sign that your hard work isn't paying off. But here's the truth: losses are not just a financial setback—they can be a powerful tax tool. When handled correctly, a business loss can reduce your tax burden in profitable years, smooth out your cash flow, and even provide a strategic advantage that your competitors overlook. As a CPA, I've seen too many owners leave money on the table simply because they didn't understand how to leverage their losses. In this guide, I'll walk you through the ins and outs of net operating losses (NOLs), the current rules that govern them, and the practical steps you can take to turn a bad year into a long-term tax win.

Understanding Net Operating Losses: The Basics

At its core, a net operating loss occurs when your business deductions exceed your business income for a given tax year. Instead of simply watching that loss disappear, the IRS allows you to use it to offset taxable income in other years. This is the fundamental concept of an NOL carryforward—you can apply the loss to future tax returns, reducing the amount of income subject to tax. For example, if your business loses $50,000 in 2025 but earns $200,000 in 2026, you can use that $50,000 loss to lower your 2026 taxable income to $150,000, potentially saving you thousands in taxes.

But the rules are not as simple as they once were. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017 made significant changes to how NOLs work, and the CARES Act temporarily altered those rules before they reverted to a modified version. Understanding the current landscape is essential for any business owner who wants to maximize the benefit of a loss.

The Evolution of NOL Rules: What Changed and What It Means for You

Before 2018, NOLs could be carried back two years and forward up to twenty years, with no limit on the amount of income they could offset. The TCJA changed that dramatically. For losses arising in tax years beginning after December 31, 2017, the carryback was eliminated (except for certain farming losses), and the carryforward became indefinite. However, the TCJA also imposed a limitation: an NOL can only offset up to 80% of taxable income in any given carryforward year. This means if you have a $100,000 NOL and your taxable income is $100,000, you can only use $80,000 of the loss in that year, leaving the remaining $20,000 to carry forward to future years.

The CARES Act, passed in 2020, temporarily suspended the 80% limitation and allowed a five-year carryback for losses arising in 2018, 2019, and 2020. But those provisions have expired. For losses incurred in 2021 and later, the rules are back to the TCJA version: no carryback, indefinite carryforward, and the 80% limit applies. It's also important to note that state tax rules can differ significantly. Some states conform to the federal rules, while others have their own carryback and carryforward provisions, and some even disallow NOLs entirely. Always check your state's specific regulations.

Strategic Ways to Use Your Business Losses

Knowing the rules is one thing; using them strategically is another. The key is to plan ahead, even when you're in the middle of a losing year. Here are some tactics I recommend to my clients:

  • Accelerate deductions into the loss year. If you anticipate a loss, consider making necessary purchases or prepaying expenses before year-end. This increases the size of the loss, which you can carry forward to offset future income. For example, if you need new equipment, buy it in the loss year to maximize your Section 179 deduction or bonus depreciation.
  • Defer income to a future year. If you have control over when you invoice clients or complete projects, consider delaying income until after the loss year. This reduces the income that the loss would offset, preserving the loss for a year when your tax rate might be higher.
  • Monitor the 80% limitation. Since you can only use 80% of your taxable income in a carryforward year, you need to project your future income. If you expect a very profitable year, you might want to use more of the loss in that year, but you'll still have to leave 20% of your income exposed. Plan accordingly to avoid surprises.
  • Consider the timing of your entity's fiscal year. If you have a C corporation, you can choose a fiscal year that aligns with your business cycle. This can help you match losses with income more effectively, though it requires careful planning and IRS approval.

These strategies require foresight, but they can turn a seemingly negative situation into a positive tax outcome.

The Interaction with Entity Structure: Who Gets the Benefit?

How your business is structured determines how losses flow through to your personal tax return. For sole proprietorships, partnerships, and S corporations, losses are passed through to the owners and can offset other income, such as wages, interest, or capital gains—subject to certain limitations. The two most important limitations are the at-risk rules and the passive activity loss rules.

The at-risk rules limit your loss deduction to the amount you have economically at risk in the business. If you invest $50,000 in your S corporation and the business loses $80,000, you can only deduct $50,000 (plus any debt you're personally liable for). The remaining $30,000 is suspended and carried forward until you have more at-risk basis.

Passive activity loss rules apply if you do not materially participate in the business. If you're a passive investor, your losses can only offset passive income, not active income like your salary. This is a common trap for limited partners or silent investors. If you're actively involved, you can usually deduct losses against any income, but you must meet the IRS's material participation tests.

For C corporations, losses are trapped at the corporate level. They can be carried forward to offset future corporate income, but they do not flow through to shareholders. This can be advantageous if you expect the corporation to be profitable in the future, but it also means you can't use the loss to offset your personal income. Choosing the right entity structure from the start—or converting when appropriate—can make a significant difference in how you utilize losses.

Common Mistakes to Avoid with Business Losses

Even with the best intentions, business owners often make errors that undermine the value of their losses. Here are the pitfalls I see most frequently:

  • Failing to track NOL carryforwards. The IRS requires you to file Form 1045 (for individuals) or Form 1139 (for corporations) to claim an NOL deduction. You also need to maintain detailed records of the loss amount, the year it occurred, and how much you've used each year. Many owners lose track of their carryforwards and miss out on deductions years later.
  • Ignoring state rules. As mentioned, states have their own NOL provisions. Some states do not allow carryforwards at all, or they have shorter carryforward periods. If you operate in multiple states, you need to compute your NOL separately for each state, which can be complex but is essential.
  • Not considering the alternative minimum tax (AMT). For individuals, the AMT can limit the benefit of NOLs. While the TCJA reduced the impact of AMT for many taxpayers, it's still a factor for high-income earners. Work with a tax professional to see if AMT applies to your situation.
  • Using losses too quickly. If you have a small loss and expect your income to grow significantly in the future, it might be better to defer using the loss until you're in a higher tax bracket. However, because the carryforward is indefinite, you have flexibility—but you must be strategic about when to claim it.

Avoiding these mistakes requires careful planning and, often, professional guidance. But the payoff can be substantial.

Planning for the Future: Turning Losses into a Long-Term Strategy

Instead of viewing a loss as a one-time event, think of it as part of your overall tax strategy. For example, if you're launching a new product line or expanding into a new market, you might intentionally incur losses in the early years, knowing that you can carry them forward to offset the profits that will eventually come. This is a legitimate tax planning technique, as long as you have a genuine profit motive and the losses are not excessive relative to your business model.

Another approach is to coordinate your loss utilization with other tax strategies, such as retirement contributions. If you have a profitable year and want to reduce your taxable income, you can use your NOL carryforward to offset that income, while also making deductible contributions to a SEP IRA or solo 401(k). The combination can dramatically lower your tax bill.

Finally, don't forget about the qualified business income (QBI) deduction. This deduction allows eligible business owners to deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income. However, the QBI deduction is limited to 20% of taxable income before the deduction. If you have an NOL carryforward, it can reduce your taxable income, which in turn may reduce your QBI deduction. This is a subtle interaction that many owners overlook. You need to model the impact to determine the optimal order of deductions.

Conclusion

A business loss is not a failure—it's an opportunity. By understanding the rules of net operating losses, planning strategically, and avoiding common pitfalls, you can turn a difficult year into a powerful tax advantage that pays off for years to come. The key is to be proactive, not reactive. Work with a qualified CPA who can help you navigate the complexities of NOLs, state variations, and entity-specific rules. With the right approach, you can ensure that every dollar of loss works as hard for you as your profits do. Remember, the tax code is not just about paying what you owe—it's about keeping what you've earned. And sometimes, that means making the most of the bad years.
