The Tax Planning Mindset: Strategic Moves to Lower Your Bill Before Year-End

2026-05-26 · Planning · Tags: taxsavingtips, The, planning

As the calendar flips toward autumn, most business owners and individuals start thinking about the holidays, not their tax returns. But the most successful taxpayers I know are the ones who treat tax planning as a year-round discipline, not a frantic scramble in April. The difference between paying thousands in unnecessary taxes and keeping that money in your pocket often comes down to decisions made in October, November, and December. This isn't about aggressive loopholes or shady tactics. It's about understanding the tax code's built-in incentives and aligning your financial moves with them before the year closes. In this guide, I'll walk you through a strategic framework to evaluate your current tax position, make smart year-end moves, and set yourself up for a significantly lower tax bill—all while staying firmly within IRS guidelines.

The Power of the Year-End Tax Projection

You can't create a strategy without knowing where you stand. Yet, I'm consistently surprised by how few taxpayers run a preliminary tax projection before December 31st. A tax projection is simply an estimate of your total tax liability for the current year, factoring in your income, deductions, and credits to date, plus your best guess for the remaining months. This isn't about preparing your actual return; it's about creating a financial map.

Why is this so critical? Because the tax code is full of thresholds, phase-outs, and deduction limits that are based on your adjusted gross income (AGI) or taxable income. For example, the qualified business income (QBI) deduction, medical expense deductions, and various education credits all have income limits. Without a projection, you're flying blind. You might miss an opportunity to reduce your income to qualify for a valuable credit, or you might inadvertently trigger the Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) or the additional Medicare tax.

To build your projection, gather your year-to-date pay stubs, business profit and loss statements, investment income reports, and records of any major life events like a marriage, a new child, or a home purchase. Use last year's tax return as a baseline and adjust for changes. If this feels overwhelming, a quick consultation with a CPA is a worthwhile investment. The goal is to identify your projected tax bracket and your proximity to key deduction phase-outs. This single piece of knowledge will inform every other decision you make before year-end.

Mastering the Art of Income Timing

Once you have a projection, you can start playing the game of income timing. The fundamental principle is simple: if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket this year than next, you want to defer income to next year. Conversely, if you expect to be in a lower bracket this year, you might want to accelerate income into the current year. This is a powerful strategy, but it requires foresight.

For employees, deferring income is often difficult, but not impossible. If you have control over a year-end bonus, you might ask your employer to pay it in January instead of December. For business owners, the options are more robust. If you use cash-basis accounting, you can delay sending invoices until late December, ensuring payment doesn't arrive until January. You can also postpone major sales or projects until the new year if it makes strategic sense. On the flip side, if you're having a low-income year, you might accelerate collections and bill early to fill up that lower tax bracket.

However, income timing isn't just about your salary or business revenue. It also applies to investment income. Consider your capital gains and losses. If you have unrealized gains in a brokerage account, you have until the last trading day of the year to decide whether to sell. If you're in a high-income year, it might be wise to hold off on selling winners until January. If you're in a low-income year, selling them now to realize the gains at a lower rate could be a smart move. The key is to be intentional, not reactive, with your investment sales.

Supercharging Your Retirement Contributions

One of the most straightforward and powerful ways to lower your tax bill is to maximize your retirement contributions. Every dollar you contribute to a traditional 401(k), a traditional IRA, or a SEP IRA reduces your taxable income dollar-for-dollar, up to the legal limits. For 2024, the 401(k) contribution limit is $23,000 (or $30,500 if you're 50 or older), and the IRA limit is $7,000 (or $8,000 if you're 50 or older). For small business owners, a SEP IRA allows contributions of up to 25% of your net earnings, capped at $69,000 for 2024.

But the strategy goes deeper than just maxing out the numbers. If you're a business owner, you have a critical decision to make: which plan is right for you? A SEP IRA is simple, but it requires you to contribute the same percentage for all eligible employees, which can be costly. A Solo 401(k) is excellent for a business with no employees other than a spouse, as it allows for both an employee deferral and a profit-sharing contribution. A defined benefit plan, or cash balance plan, is a powerful tool for high-earning business owners in their 40s and 50s, as it can allow for contributions of $200,000 or more per year, dramatically reducing taxable income.

The key is to set up these plans before December 31st to make contributions for the current tax year. While you can make contributions to a SEP IRA or a Solo 401(k) up until the tax filing deadline (usually April 15th), the plan itself must be established by the end of the calendar year. This is a classic year-end planning move that many people miss. Don't let another year slip by without taking advantage of this powerful, legal tax shelter.

Strategic Deduction Bunching and Prepayment

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) significantly raised the standard deduction, which made itemizing less beneficial for many taxpayers. For 2024, the standard deduction is $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married couples filing jointly. This means you only benefit from itemizing if your total deductible expenses—like state and local taxes, mortgage interest, and charitable contributions—exceed that threshold. This is where the strategy of 'bunching' comes into play.

Bunching is the practice of concentrating your deductible expenses into a single tax year to clear the standard deduction hurdle, while taking the standard deduction in the following year. For example, instead of making a $5,000 charitable contribution every year, you could make a $10,000 contribution every other year. In the year you make the large contribution, you itemize and get a big deduction. In the 'off' year, you take the standard deduction. This can result in a lower total tax bill over two years than if you had simply made smaller contributions each year and never itemized.

You can also bunch other expenses. If you have control over your property tax payments, you might pay two years' worth in a single year (if your state allows it). You can also prepay your January mortgage payment in December to capture the interest deduction a year earlier. For medical expenses, which are deductible only to the extent they exceed 7.5% of your AGI, you can schedule elective procedures or purchase necessary medical equipment in a year when you know your medical costs will be high. This is a powerful way to turn a large, unexpected expense into a tax-saving opportunity.

Leveraging Business Entity Choices and Expenses

For small business owners, the structure of your entity has a massive impact on your tax bill. If you're a sole proprietor or a single-member LLC, your business income is reported on your personal tax return, and you're subject to self-employment tax on the entire net profit. If you're an S-corporation, you can pay yourself a 'reasonable salary' and take the rest of the profits as distributions, which are not subject to self-employment tax. This can save you thousands of dollars each year, but it requires careful planning and payroll administration.

Beyond entity structure, the year-end is the perfect time to review your business expenses and ensure you're capturing every deduction you're entitled to. This isn't about inventing expenses; it's about properly documenting and timing the ones you already have. For example, if you need new equipment, software, or office furniture, purchasing it before December 31st can allow you to take advantage of Section 179 or bonus depreciation, which lets you deduct the full cost in the year of purchase. This is a powerful way to reduce your taxable income while investing in your business's future.

Don't overlook the smaller, everyday expenses either. Are you tracking your mileage for business travel? Are you properly documenting your home office deduction? Are you separating business meals from entertainment (which is no longer deductible)? A thorough year-end review of your profit and loss statement can uncover missed deductions that are hiding in plain sight. Look for any expenses that were paid for with personal funds but are business-related, and make sure to reimburse yourself from the business account before year-end. This not only cleans up your books but also ensures you get the tax benefit.

The Art of Tax-Loss Harvesting in Your Portfolio

While we all hope for a year of strong investment gains, the reality is that markets are volatile. If you have investments that have lost value, you can turn that negative into a strategic positive through tax-loss harvesting. The idea is simple: sell the losing investments to realize the capital loss, which can then be used to offset any capital gains you've realized during the year. If your losses exceed your gains, you can use up to $3,000 of the excess loss to offset your ordinary income, and any remaining losses can be carried forward to future years.

This strategy is most effective when you're in a high tax bracket, as the savings from offsetting gains can be substantial. However, it's crucial to be aware of the 'wash-sale' rule. This rule prohibits you from claiming a loss on a security if you buy a 'substantially identical' security within 30 days before or after the sale. If you do, the loss is disallowed, and your tax basis is adjusted. To avoid this, you can either wait 31 days to repurchase the same stock or immediately buy a different, but similar, fund or stock to maintain your market exposure.

Tax-loss harvesting is not just a December activity; it should be monitored throughout the year. But the year-end is a critical time to review your portfolio for any unrealized losses. If you have a winner you want to sell, you can also use this strategy to 'pair' it with a loser to neutralize the tax impact. This is a sophisticated but highly effective way to manage your investment tax bill and keep more of your returns working for you.

Conclusion

Tax planning is not a single event; it's a mindset. It's about being proactive, understanding the rules, and making intentional decisions throughout the year. By running a year-end projection, timing your income, maximizing retirement contributions, bunching your deductions, reviewing your business structure, and strategically harvesting losses, you can significantly reduce your tax liability. These aren't just abstract concepts; they are concrete, actionable steps that can put thousands of dollars back into your pocket. The time to act is now, before the year closes. Take control of your financial future, and you'll find that tax season becomes a time of opportunity, not anxiety.
